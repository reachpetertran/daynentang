Grown-ups like to tell their children: "If something feels wrong, just come home." That is true. But it leaves something out: for a fourteen-year-old, standing up and leaving in front of friends can be harder than staying.
Some rooms have no lock on the door and still cannot be left. What holds a person there is a few pairs of eyes.
In a small town in the hill country of the eastern United States lived a boy named Caleb, fourteen years old. That spring, after dinner one evening, his father pushed his plate aside and said:
— Let's have a family code. Any time, anywhere, if you find you don't want to be where you are any more, you text me or your mother one single letter: X.
— And then what?
— Then I call you and say something has come up at home and I have to pick you up right away. Blame it all on me. Your friends will think you have an annoying dad. That's all.
Caleb shrugged. It seemed a bit childish to him.
— There's one more thing, — his mother said. — On the way home, we won't ask you anything. You tell us as much as you want. Or nothing at all.
— Even if I'm somewhere I'm not allowed to be?
His parents looked at each other. This was the hardest part, and the two of them had talked it over for a week.
— Even then, — his father said. — With one exception: if someone there is still in danger, you have to tell us.
Caleb said okay to get it over with, and forgot all about it.
*
One Saturday night in October, Caleb slept over at the house of Ryan, a classmate. Ryan's parents were away. At nine, Ryan's older brother came home with four older friends, carrying a few packs of beer and something passed from hand to hand in a paper bag.
The music grew louder. A can of beer was pushed into Caleb's hand.
— Drink up, kid. Or do you have to ask Mummy first?
The whole room laughed. Caleb laughed along, because not laughing would have looked odd. He held the ice-cold can without opening it. He looked at the door. From where he sat to the door was six steps. Those six steps led past every person in the room.
He knew he ought to go. He also knew exactly what would be said behind his back on Monday morning.
In the bathroom, Caleb stood looking at his phone for a long time. Then he typed one letter, and sent it.

Two minutes later the phone rang in the middle of the living room. Caleb answered, making sure everyone could hear.
— Caleb, — his father's voice was urgent. — Something's come up at home. I have to pick you up right now. I'll be there in five minutes.
— What's happened, Dad?
— I'll tell you in the truck. Go out to the gate.
Caleb hung up and pulled an annoyed face.
— My old man. No idea what it is this time.
— That sucks, — said Ryan.
Nobody laughed. Nobody called him kid. The six steps to the door were suddenly as light as walking through his own house.
*
His father's pickup was already at the gate with the engine running. Caleb climbed in, fastened his seat belt and looked straight ahead. He waited. The first question was bound to be: what was going on in there?
His father asked only two things.
— Are you all right?
— I'm all right.
— Is anybody in there in danger?
Caleb thought of Ryan, and of the cans of beer.
— No. It's just… I didn't want to stay.
His father nodded and put the truck in gear. The whole way home he did not ask one more question. Caleb glanced across and saw his father's hands gripping the wheel so hard the knuckles were white. He understood that his father had a hundred questions, and was holding back all hundred.
Keeping a promise when it is easy hardly counts as keeping it. That night, over fifteen minutes of road, a father kept his word by staying silent.
At home, his mother only said, "Are you hungry?" and warmed up the pot of soup.
On Sunday afternoon, Caleb came down to the kitchen on his own and sat in his usual chair.
— About last night, — he said. — Can I tell you?
His parents turned off the stove and sat down. Caleb told them everything, even the part where he laughed along when he was teased. Nobody interrupted. When he had finished, he asked:
— Are you going to punish me?
— For what? — his father said. — For knowing your way home?
An emergency exit is only useful when a person is sure that stepping through it will not earn a scolding. Every child will one day wander into a room they should not be in. What parents can do is leave a door ready, and keep that door open, with a promise they dare to keep.
The letter X is only two strokes crossing each other. But that night it said, in place of a fourteen-year-old boy, a very long sentence he did not know how to say: "Dad, come and get me."
