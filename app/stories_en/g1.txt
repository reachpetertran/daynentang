Every home has laws, even a home that has never written down a word. The laws live in a father's frown, in a mother's sigh, in every "we don't do that in this family". Children learn those laws the way they learn to walk: by stumbling.
There are things every family believes it holds dear. Only when they have to write down four lines do people find out they have never sat down and asked one another.
On the edge of a seaside city in the Philippines there is a narrow alley where the power lines hang like a spider's web. Halfway down the alley is a shop no bigger than a window, selling coffee by the sachet and eggs one at a time. The shop belongs to Bea's family.
Bea's father, Ramon, drives passengers from before the sun is up. Her mother, Liza, minds the shop and the kitchen at once. Bea is nine. Her little brother Jun has just turned four, the age when everything is followed by "why".
One Sunday evening, Mother switched off the television and put a piece of cardboard cut from a noodle box in the middle of the table, with a marker pen beside it.
— Fifteen minutes, that's all, — she said. — We're having a family meeting.
— A meeting about what? — Bea asked. — I didn't do anything wrong.
— Nobody did anything wrong. I only want to ask: what does our family treasure most?
Jun's hand shot up first:
— Ice cream!
Everyone laughed. Then Father said "working hard". Mother said "telling the truth". Bea thought for a while and said "keeping your word", with a sideways look at her father, who had promised a trip to the beach three weeks ago. Father scratched his head and added: "helping each other".
Mother wrote four lines on the cardboard. Her handwriting was not pretty, and the marker bled. Father taped it to the kitchen door, level with Bea's eyes.
— So we stick it up and that's it? — Bea asked.
— No. Sticking it up is where it starts.
*
That Thursday the heat sat heavy on the alley. The shop was busy from noon. At dusk Mother counted the money in the biscuit tin while Bea sat beside her, stacking the coins into little towers.
— Mama, there's a hundred extra today.
Mother counted again. A hundred pesos too many. She remembered at once: old Mrs Cora from the end of the alley had bought rice and handed over two notes stuck together. The customers had been crowding in, and Mother had not had time to look.
A hundred pesos is no small thing to a shop that sells eggs one at a time. This month's electricity was still unpaid. Mother held the note and was quiet for longer than usual.
— Never mind, — she said, her voice tired. — She doesn't know either. We'll see about it tomorrow.
Bea said nothing. She only turned her head towards the kitchen door. There, the second line on the cardboard was written in her mother's own hand.
Children do not argue with their parents by reasoning. They argue by remembering.
Mother followed her daughter's eyes. She saw the cardboard. She also saw a nine-year-old girl waiting to find out whether those four lines were real, or only something to stick on a door.
There are times when doing the right thing costs exactly one hundred pesos. Not doing it costs more, only the cost cannot be counted in money.
Mother stood up and slipped on her sandals.
— Bea, come with me.
The two of them walked to the end of the alley. Mrs Cora was washing vegetables and looked up in surprise. She took the hundred-peso note, looked at Mother, then at Bea.
— I didn't even know I'd lost it, — she said. — You walked it all the way to my door. From now on I buy my rice at your shop and nowhere else.
On the way home, Mother held Bea's hand more tightly than on other days.
— I nearly did the wrong thing back there, — she said. — Thank you for looking at the cardboard.
— But I didn't say anything.
— No. You didn't need to.
*
The next Sunday evening, the family met again. This time Father began:
— This week I want to praise your mother and Bea. For telling the truth, even when the truth cost us.
Then he hesitated.
— As for me, I owe a promise. Next Sunday I'll take the morning off driving. We are going to the beach.
Jun jumped up and down. Bea did not jump. She took the marker and drew a small star beside the line "keeping your word", for her father.
Before the meeting ended, Jun insisted on writing too. He could not write yet, so he drew. In the bottom corner of the cardboard he drew an ice cream as big as all four lines put together. Nobody had the heart to rub it out.
The cardboard is still on the kitchen door. It has yellowed from the cooking oil, and one corner of the tape has come loose. But every week it gains another star, sometimes for one person, sometimes for another.
A family's values are not in neat handwriting or in how high they hang. They are in the evening when someone is willing to slip on her sandals and walk to the end of the alley, so that a child can see the words on the cardboard and her mother's feet going the same way.
