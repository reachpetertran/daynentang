A child who cannot talk yet talks all day long. He talks with his eyes, with a pointing finger, with babble that forms no words. Each time, he is throwing a small ball and waiting to see whether anyone will throw it back.
Some balls are thrown back. Others drop to the floor, roll into a corner and lie there.
In the mid-1980s, in a poor neighbourhood of Kingston, Jamaica, there lived a young mother named Marva. She lived in a room with a zinc roof with her son Devon, eighteen months old. Devon was smaller than other children his age. He seldom smiled, and he hardly spoke.
Marva loved her son in the way she knew: feeding him as well as she could, bathing him clean, sitting him down in one spot so that she could get on with the washing she took in. Nobody had ever told her that a baby needs to be talked to. And what do you say to someone who cannot talk?
One Tuesday morning, someone knocked on the door frame. It was Miss Pearl, the community health worker, a face the whole neighbourhood knew. That day she carried no scales and no medicine. She carried a cloth bag.
— I've come to play with Devon for an hour, — she said. — Sit and play with us.
Marva almost laughed out loud. A grown woman, at work, coming to play with a baby?
Miss Pearl sat right down on the floor. Out of the bag she took an old plastic bottle with a few coloured bottle caps inside. She shook it. The bottle rattled.
Devon looked. He did not reach out. He only looked, and made a very soft sound: "Uh."
— Uh! — Miss Pearl answered at once, her eyes wide as if she had just heard great news. — You heard that, did you? It goes rattle-rattle. Rattle-rattle!
Devon looked at her. Then he looked at his mother. Marva sat still, hands on her knees, not knowing what to do.
— Did you see? — Miss Pearl said quietly. — He just asked you something. Answer him.
— What do I say?
— Say what you see. "This is a bottle." "This is a red cap." Then wait for him.
Marva picked up the bottle, awkwardly, as if it belonged to someone else.
— Bottle… this is a bottle, Devon. — She gave it a shake. — Rattle-rattle.
Devon leaned towards her. For the first time that morning, he smiled.
*
At the end of the hour, Miss Pearl left the bottle behind.
— Next Tuesday I'll come back and swap it for something else. Until then, play with him a little every day. When you do the washing, tell him what you are washing. When he manages something, praise him.
— Is that all?
— That's all. But every day.
The next Tuesday the bottle was swapped for some wooden blocks. The Tuesday after, for a picture book pasted together from cardboard, one picture to a page: a hen, a banana, a spoon. Every toy was made from things people throw away. Every one stayed only a week.
What stayed longer was Marva's voice. She began talking to her son while she carried water, while she picked over the greens. "This is water. Cold, isn't it?" At first she felt like someone talking to herself. Then one afternoon Devon pointed at the basin and said, quite clearly: "Wa-wa."
Marva dropped the shirt she was wringing.
That Tuesday she told Miss Pearl before the woman could even sit down. Miss Pearl listened, and did not praise Devon first. She praised Marva.
— That is your doing. I only brought the bottle.
Nobody had ever told Marva she was good at being a mother. People only ever told her she was poor.
*
Miss Pearl came like that for two years, one hour a week, one bag. Then the programme ended. The bag did not come back. Devon grew up, went to school, went to work, like every other child in the neighbourhood.
Twenty years later, researchers went looking for the babies of those Tuesdays, now grown. They compared the ones the cloth bag had visited with others from the same streets and the same hardship whom it had not. On average, the visited group was earning about a quarter more.
None of them remembered what colour the caps in the bottle had been. Memory does not keep the first years of life. But something else had kept them.
People tend to think that giving a child a better life means having money to buy the child something. The story from Kingston says otherwise. The most precious thing a child needs is something even the poorest home has: a grown-up willing to sit down on the floor and throw the ball back.
