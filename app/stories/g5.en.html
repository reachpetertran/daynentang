The Teapot at Nine O'Clock

Told by Liêm

WhereA port city in western Türkiye
ObjectA double teapot
Reading ageFor the whole family, from age 8 · about 12 minutes
CourseParents as one team (course in Vietnamese)

Invented story. The characters, the place and every event were made up by the writer, based on the content of the course. The story is not modelled on any real family.

Children listen with their whole bodies. Their parents may speak low behind a closed door, and the child still hears it in a spoon set down too hard, in a dinner where nobody looks at anybody. Children do not understand all the words. They understand the weather.

In some homes the weather turns without warning. And the child who lives there learns to read the sky before learning to read.

In a port city on the western coast of Türkiye, in a third-floor flat with a balcony looking down on the market, lived a family of three. Murat repaired engines at the shipyard. Elif sold cloth. Their daughter Defne was six, and loved to draw.

Murat and Elif loved each other. But they were raising their child in two different ways. He was easy-going, she was strict. She said bed at eight; he allowed one more episode. She said we are not buying that; he bought it on the quiet. Each time, she felt she had become the villain of the house. And each time she said so, he felt lectured like a second child.

That evening it started with a packet of sweets.

— I had just told her no, — Elif said. — You were standing right there.

— It's only a packet of sweets. You always make such a big thing of it.

— And you always leave me to be the mean one.

Their voices rose. Neither noticed when Defne left the table.

When Elif went into her daughter's room, the girl was already asleep, with dried tear tracks on her cheeks. On the desk lay a drawing. A house. A black pencil line down the very middle, from the roof to the floor. On the left, one grown-up. On the right, one grown-up. And the child standing right on the line, one foot on each side.

Elif carried the paper to the kitchen and laid it in front of her husband. Murat looked at it for a long time.

— We argue because of her, — he said, his voice rough. — And we are cutting her in two.

* * *

That night they did not go back to the packet of sweets. Elif put the double teapot on the stove, the kind every home here has: water boiling below, strong tea above. She poured two small tulip-shaped glasses.

— I don't want to beat you, — she said. — I want us on the same team.

They sat up late, and agreed on three things.

One: no arguing in front of their child. When one person's voice begins to rise, the other need only say two words: "Nine o'clock." Meaning: this is real, but it waits until nine, when the child is asleep and the teapot is boiling.

Two: at nine sharp, whoever speaks first must open with a thank-you. Only then the matter itself. And say "I feel", "I think", never "you always", "you never".

Three: in front of Defne, her parents speak with one voice. Once one of them has answered the child, the other keeps to that answer. Any disagreement waits until nine.

— Do you think we can manage it? — Murat asked.

— I don't know. But tea we can make every night.

* * *

Three days later, the third rule was tested.

— Daddy, can I watch one more episode? — Defne whispered, with a glance towards the kitchen. — Mummy said no, but if you say yes it's all right.

Murat opened his mouth. The word "yes" was already on the tip of his tongue, because it was the easiest word. He swallowed it.

— Mummy has answered, sweetheart, so I say the same.

Defne's eyes went round. She pouted for a while, then went to brush her teeth. The strange thing was that she did not cry. Children push against a wall to see whether it will fall. When the wall holds, they stop pushing, and lean on it in peace.

Nine o'clock. The teapot boiled.

— Thank you for this evening, — Elif said first. — I know how hard it is for you to say no to her.

— Thank you for not bringing up the sweets again, — Murat answered. — Here is what I think. Eight is a little early for a Friday. At weekends, could we let her stay up until half past eight?

Elif thought for a moment, then nodded. For the first time in many months, a conversation about their child ended with nobody feeling they had lost.

Not every evening went as smoothly. Some evenings the words "nine o'clock" came out through clenched teeth. Some evenings the tea went cold before the talk was done. But grown-up matters now had an hour of their own, and they no longer spilled over into a child's dinner.

A month later, Defne drew a new picture and stuck it on the fridge herself. The same house. No black line. In a lit window two grown-ups sat facing each other, and between them she had drawn a teapot, nearly as big as the table.

Every couple disagrees sometimes. A child does not need her parents to be exactly alike. She only needs to know that those two grown-ups stand on the same side, and that every night, after she has gone to sleep, a teapot in the kitchen is keeping the house from cracking in two.

The object in this story

A double teapot

  • What it does: keeps a fixed hour for grown-ups to talk about hard things, once the child is asleep.
  • Three things that go with it: no arguing in front of the child; begin with a thank-you; speak with one voice in front of the child.
  • If your home does not drink tea: a glass of water, an agreed hour and two words to remind each other are enough.

Talk together

  1. What did Defne's first drawing tell her parents?
  2. Why did Defne not cry when Father said the same as Mother?
  3. How is our family like Defne's? When the grown-ups disagree, how does it feel to you?

One thing to do this week

When one person's voice begins to rise, the two of us will say "tonight", then go on with the talk after the child is asleep, opening with a thank-you.
