The Yellow Can That Takes a Bow

Told by Liêm

WhereA town near Lake Volta, eastern Ghana
ObjectA handwashing tap made from a plastic can
Reading ageRead together from age 4 · about 12 minutes
CourseFamily 2–8: The Foundation Hand

Based on a true story. A seven-year-old girl saw a handwashing station at school, went home and kept asking until her family had one, and became the youngest handwashing monitor in her school. Names have been changed. The dialogue, the grandmother and many details were added by the writer.

Our hands are the thing we use most and look at least. They scoop up rice, lead a little brother along, pick up a fallen mango, get wiped in a hurry on the hem of a shirt. Over one day a pair of hands goes to so many places that hardly anyone remembers them all.

Some things are so small that grown-ups forget them. And some children are so small that they refuse to forget.

In the east of Ghana, near a wide lake called Volta, there is a town of red earth. A seven-year-old girl named Dzifa lived there. She lived with her father and mother, with her grandmother, and with her little brother Kofi, just turned three, who trotted after his sister wherever she went.

One morning, something strange had sprung up in Dzifa's schoolyard. Two forked wooden posts, a crossbar resting on top, and hanging in the middle a yellow plastic jerrycan. From the neck of the can a string ran down to a stick lying close to the ground. Beside it, a bar of soap on a cord swung in the wind.

The teacher put her foot on the stick. The can tipped forward like someone taking a bow, and a thin stream of water ran out.

— Your hands never need to touch the can, — she said. — Foot down, water runs. Rub the soap in well, then rinse. Before you eat. After the toilet. That is all there is to it.

The children lined up, every one of them wanting a turn. When hers came, Dzifa pressed the stick three times. She looked at the water, at her two wet hands, then back at the can. A question was already waiting in her head: why don't we have one at home?

That afternoon she went home and said to her father:

— Papa, make me a tippy-tap.

Father was mending his net and did not look up:

— We already have a basin, child.

— With a basin everybody dips into the same water. Teacher says the water has to run for hands to get clean.

— All right. Tomorrow.

Grown-ups have a day called "tomorrow". It is a very roomy day. Everything not yet done fits inside it.

The next day, Dzifa asked again. Father said tomorrow. The day after, she asked again. Mother said her father was busy. Dzifa did not cry, and she did not sulk. She simply did the one thing children are better at than grown-ups: she did not stop.

She walked along the hedge and brought home two forked branches. She asked her grandmother for the empty cooking-oil can. Grandmother rinsed it three times, set it out in the sun, and asked:

— What are you going to do with it?

— I'm going to teach it to wash hands.

Grandmother laughed until her shoulders shook. But that evening it was she who spoke to Dzifa's father:

— The child has been asking you for four days now. Anything a child brings up for the fourth time is a thing worth doing.

* * *

On Saturday morning, Father put down his net. He dug two holes under the mango tree and set the two posts in firmly. He heated a nail and pierced a small hole near the neck of the can. That part he did alone, and no child was allowed near it.

Dzifa tied the strings. She tied the first to the neck of the can and the second to the bar of soap. Kofi was given the most important job of all, according to his sister: to stand and watch and not fiddle.

By late morning it was done. The yellow can hung under the mango tree, filled to its shoulder.

— Who washes first? — Father asked.

— Grandma, — said Dzifa. — Because the can is hers.

Grandmother put her foot on the stick. The can took its bow. Water ran over two hands that for decades had scooped rice, scrubbed clothes and carried grandchildren. She rubbed the soap in for a long time, like someone looking closely at her own hands for the first time.

Then Mother. Then Father. Kofi washed last and longest, because he liked watching the can bow.

From that day on, the house had a small gatekeeper. Before each meal Dzifa stood beside the can, and nobody got past without washing. Father came back from the lake, and washed. Mother came back from the market, and washed. One day Father was tired and made to sit straight down to eat. Dzifa said nothing. She just stood still beside the can and looked at him. Father sighed, and got up.

A seven-year-old cannot give orders to anyone. But when a seven-year-old stands still and waits, few grown-ups can go on sitting.

* * *

The neighbour women came by, found it curious and asked to see. Dzifa showed each of them, just as her teacher had shown her: foot down, water runs, rub well, then rinse. Before long the house on the left had a green can. The house on the right had a white one. The children of the lane called them "the cans that bow".

At school, the teacher heard the story. She called Dzifa to the front of the class and gave her a job: to be the one who reminds the others to wash their hands. She was the youngest child in the school ever given that job.

Dzifa was only seven. She could not yet read all the words in her books. But she already knew something no book finds easy to teach: if a thing is right and nobody has done it, you do it. And if they tell you tomorrow, then tomorrow you ask again.

Some changes begin with a big meeting. Others begin with an empty cooking-oil can, two forked branches, and a child who will not stop.

The object in this story

A handwashing tap made from a plastic can

  • What it does: a foot presses the stick, the can tips, water runs. Dirty hands never touch the can.
  • What you need: a clean plastic can, two posts, a crossbar, string and soap.
  • If your home has a tap: no can needed. Only soap, and remembering to wash before eating and after the toilet.

Talk together

  1. Dzifa's family already had a basin. Why did she still want a tap made from a can?
  2. What did Grandmother say to Father? Why do you think Father changed his mind?
  3. How is our family like Dzifa's? Is there something our family keeps leaving "for tomorrow"?

One thing to do this week

When the family is about to sit down to dinner, we will wash our hands with soap under running water. The child is the one who reminds us.

Safety

Anything with knives, nails or fire is a job for grown-ups.

The water in the can is only for washing hands, not for drinking.

Young children wash their hands with a grown-up standing by.
